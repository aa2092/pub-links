RESEARCHERS ARE INVESTIGATING THE IRON + ADHD CONNECTION. 1/6

1/6 – RESEARCHERS ARE INVESTIGATING THE IRON + ADHD CONNECTION. 1/6

2/6 – 1. IRON HELPS YOUR BRAIN MAKE DOPAMINE. Dopamine plays a role in motivation, attention, and reward processing. Your body uses an iron-dependent enzyme called tyrosine hydroxylase to produce it. That's one reason researchers are interested in how iron status might relate to brain function. 2/6

3/6 – 2. FERRITIN MEASURES YOUR STORED IRON. You can have depleted iron stores before developing obvious iron-deficiency anemia. Ferritin is one of the main blood tests clinicians use to assess iron stores, although inflammation can sometimes make ferritin appear higher than expected. 3/6

4/6 – 3. SOME STUDIES HAVE FOUND LOWER FERRITIN LEVELS IN CHILDREN WITH ADHD. Researchers have reported lower average ferritin levels in some groups of children with ADHD compared with children without ADHD. However, not every study finds the same result, and these findings don't establish that iron deficiency causes ADHD. 4/6

5/6 – 4. LOW IRON CAN CAUSE SYMPTOMS THAT OVERLAP WITH ADHD. Iron deficiency can contribute to fatigue, weakness, and difficulty concentrating. These symptoms may resemble or worsen some attention-related difficulties, but they can also have many other explanations. 5/6

6/6 – 5. THIS DOESN'T MEAN EVERYONE WITH ADHD NEEDS IRON SUPPLEMENTS. Iron is essential, but taking extra iron when you aren't deficient won't necessarily improve attention or dopamine function. Too much iron can be harmful, so testing and treatment should be guided by a healthcare professional. 6/6

Claim #A2·Supported·High confidence
Research Quality: Claim: ferritin reflects iron stores, which can run low before anaemia, and inflammation raises ferritin. All three points match the WHO 2020 guideline [1] and a Lancet seminar [2] (GRADE moderate, strong consensus). The only caveat is that cut-offs vary by guideline and age.

Research Quality: Claim: not everyone with ADHD needs iron, extra iron without deficiency won't help, and testing should come first. A Level 1 review of 3 small RCTs found no significant benefit and flagged fatal overdose risk [1], and benefits disappear without deficiency [2] (GRADE low, strong consensus on "test, then treat"). The cautious claim matches this. The often-cited pilot (n=23, 5 on placebo) is at high risk of bias [3]; larger RCTs in iron-deficient children with ADHD would help.

About this report

10 claims re-evaluated with the Scientific Evidence Evaluator framework. The iron thread is careful and well hedged. The consistency tips are sensible but rest on general habit research, not ADHD trials. One verdict changed: A4 (iron and concentration) moved to Partially supported because RCTs show no effect of iron on attention.

How claims were evaluated. Evidence hierarchy: Systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Level 1), RCTs (2), cohort (3), case-control (4), cross-sectional (5), case reports and expert opinion (6). Guidelines and clinical reviews are named as such. GRADE certainty: Our rating of the body of evidence for this specific claim: randomised evidence starts High and observational starts Low. Ratings go down for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness (e.g. non-ADHD or lab-only samples), imprecision and publication bias, and up for large effects. Verdicts: Supported: strong, consistent, high-quality evidence. Partially supported: some solid evidence, but the claim overgeneralises or has important caveats. Insufficient evidence: too little good-quality research to judge. Unsupported: evidence is absent for the claim as stated, or contradicts it (flagged as contradicted). Confidence is our confidence in the verdict, which can be high even when GRADE is low (e.g. a well-hedged claim).

Verdict changes: 1 changed in substance; 4 relabelled onto the four-category scale with no change in substance (Not supported or Contradicted → Unsupported; Partly → Partially supported).
