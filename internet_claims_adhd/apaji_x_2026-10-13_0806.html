3/7 – The first reason is hyperfocus. When you are absorbed in a task, internal signals like thirst, hunger and a full bladder get filtered out. They are still being sent, but they do not reach your awareness until they become impossible to ignore. 3/7

5/7 – Here is the part that surprises people: thirst is a late signal. By the time you feel thirsty, you may already be mildly dehydrated. Even mild dehydration has been linked in studies to worse concentration, mood and headaches, which can feel just like an ADHD flare-up. 5/7

6/7 – Caffeine makes it trickier. Coffee and energy drinks can keep you alert and cover up tiredness, so you do not notice that you are running low on fluid. Coffee still counts toward your fluids, but it should not be the only thing you drink. 6/7

1/7 – Poor sleep and a restless gut often show up together, and most people never connect the two. For ADHD brains, which already struggle with sleep, this link matters even more. Here is what the research says, step by step. 1/7

2/7 – Start with the basics. Your gut has its own daily rhythm. Digestion, gut movement and even the activity of gut bacteria follow patterns tied to your body clock. This is why eating at wildly different times can leave your gut feeling off. 2/7

3/7 – Sleep affects the gut first. Studies have found that short or disrupted sleep is linked with changes in gut bacteria and with more digestive complaints like bloating and irregularity. Much of this research is observational, so it shows an association, not proof of cause. 3/7

4/7 – Now the other direction, which surprises people. Your gut also seems to affect sleep. Gut bacteria help produce compounds that interact with the nervous system, and researchers are studying whether they influence sleep quality. This work is still early, with much of it in animals and small human studies. 4/7

5/7 – Here is a point many posts get wrong. You may have heard that serotonin made in the gut turns into melatonin, the sleep hormone, and sends you to bed. The truth is more complicated. Gut serotonin mostly works locally and does not simply cross into the brain, so be cautious about claims that a gut fix will give you better sleep. 5/7

6/7 – Then there is the loop. Poor sleep raises stress and cravings for sugary, starchy food. That kind of eating can upset digestion, and an upset gut can make sleep lighter. Add ADHD, with its late body clock and busy mind at night, and the loop gets easier to fall into. 6/7

7/7 – What helps is simple and low-risk. Eat meals at fairly regular times, finish dinner a few hours before bed, add fiber from oats, beans, fruit and vegetables, and include fermented foods like yogurt or kefir if they suit you. Keep caffeine to the morning and early afternoon, and keep a steady wake time. 7/7

About this report

19 new claims and 6 repeat cards from 15 threads, checked with the Scientific Evidence Evaluator framework. The careful, hedged threads (sleep and the gut, hydration, the orexin drug, rejection sensitivity) mostly held up, and coffee counting toward fluids and ADHD's link with poor sleep and a later body clock were well supported. The confident mechanism claims did worst again: "Visual Satiation Paralysis" is a made-up label, "the ADHD brain can't feel mess" has no study behind it, and the dopamine-deficit story (now applied to procrastination, crises, novelty and spending) repeats claim 9 from 11 Oct in its strongest, unsupported form. The sugar thread cites a real Nature Microbiology paper, but the causal chain was shown only in mice. Two threads end in product promotions (a diabetes bundle and a paid "ADHD Reset Protocol").

How claims were evaluated. Evidence hierarchy: Systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Level 1), RCTs (2), cohort (3), case-control (4), cross-sectional (5), case reports and expert opinion (6). Guidelines and clinical reviews are named as such. GRADE certainty: Our rating of the body of evidence for this specific claim: randomised evidence starts High and observational starts Low. Ratings go down for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness (e.g. non-ADHD or lab-only samples), imprecision and publication bias, and up for large effects. Verdicts: Supported: strong, consistent, high-quality evidence. Partially supported: some solid evidence, but the claim overgeneralises or has important caveats. Insufficient evidence: too little good-quality research to judge. Unsupported: evidence is absent for the claim as stated, or contradicts it (flagged as contradicted). Confidence is our confidence in the verdict, which can be high even when GRADE is low (e.g. a well-hedged claim).

Verdict changes: None. No new part extended a thread checked earlier, so no earlier verdict was re-assessed. Repeat cards point back to the 11 and 12 Oct verdicts, which still stand; R6 rates a softer limerence post Insufficient evidence instead of C19's Unsupported because it drops the dopamine claim.

Method. Posts were pulled with the X API. Self-reply parts were reconstructed via conversation_id and reply links and quoted verbatim, numbered in order. Reference metadata, corrections and retractions were checked against Crossref and PubMed, and open-access full texts were read. GRADE ratings are our own per-claim judgement unless stated.
