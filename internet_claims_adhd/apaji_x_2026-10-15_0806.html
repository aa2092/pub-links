Claim #F1·Supported·High confidence
Point 1 Supported High confidence

A 2025 Nature study found rare variants in MAP1A, ANO8 and ANK2 that raise the odds of ADHD up to about 15-fold.

Research Quality: Accurately reported: an exome-sequencing study of 8,895 people with ADHD and 53,780 controls found these three genes with odds ratios 5.55–15.13 [1] (Level 4 case-control, very large n, exome-wide significance). Caveats the post omits: the figure is an odds ratio for carriers of very rare variants, so these genes explain only a small share of ADHD cases, and independent replication is still pending. Replication in non-Nordic cohorts would strengthen it.
Point 2 Supported High confidence

ADHD is one of the most heritable psychiatric conditions, at around 74%.

Research Quality: The 74% figure is the mean twin-study heritability in the field's standard review [2], echoed by the international consensus statement [3] (twin/family evidence, consistent across many cohorts). Heritability is a population statistic, not a per-person probability, and common variants explain about a third of it. Consensus is high.
Point 3 Supported Moderate confidence

Parents and relatives with lifelong 'scattered' traits often have unrecognised ADHD that runs in the family.

Research Quality: Family, twin and adoption studies all show ADHD aggregates in families [2], so a parent recognising their own traits at a child's assessment fits the evidence. How often such parents are undiagnosed is not measured in the sources checked, and shared environments also contribute. Studies screening parents at child assessments would strengthen it.

Claim #F2·Partially supported·Moderate confidence
Point 1 Insufficient evidence Moderate confidence

'Waiting mode': an appointment later in the day stops people with ADHD starting or enjoying anything beforehand (also the 4 pm appointment post).

Research Quality: A widely shared lived experience, but no peer-reviewed study of 'waiting mode' was found in PubMed or Crossref; it may overlap with time-perception and prospective-memory difficulties, which are only partly established (see 12 Oct C3). A diary or experience-sampling study comparing ADHD and control adults on appointment days would test it.
Point 2 Partially supported Moderate confidence

'Revenge bedtime procrastination': staying up late because night is the only time that feels your own is an ADHD experience.

Research Quality: ADHD is linked to a later body clock and sleep problems [1], and inattention correlates with general procrastination in a small student sample [2] (GRADE very low for the bedtime form). No study in diagnosed ADHD of the 'reclaiming my time' motive was verified. Studies using the Bedtime Procrastination Scale in diagnosed adults would strengthen it.
Point 3 Partially supported Moderate confidence

'Hyperfocus hangover': hours of absorption, forgetting to eat or drink, then exhaustion.

Research Quality: Adults with more ADHD symptoms report hyperfocus more often [3], and reduced body-signal awareness has been reported in ADHD [4], but hyperfocus has no agreed definition or validated measure [5], and the after-effect 'hangover' is unstudied. Validated hyperfocus measures with objective lapses in eating or drinking would strengthen it.
Point 4 Insufficient evidence Moderate confidence

'Reply guilt': forgetting to answer a message, then guilt making replying harder.

Research Quality: Plausible and consistent with avoidance/shame themes covered earlier, but no study of message-reply avoidance in ADHD was found. Experience-sampling research on communication avoidance would test it.

Claim #F6·Partially supported·Moderate confidence
Point 1 Partially supported Moderate confidence

Needing a podcast or background sound while doing chores helps the ADHD brain work.

Research Quality: A meta-analysis found white/pink noise gave a small benefit on tasks for children with ADHD symptoms [1], and benefit varies between individuals [2] (GRADE low). Podcasts are speech, not noise, and were not tested. Trials of speech vs noise vs silence during routine tasks would strengthen it.
Point 2 Unsupported Moderate confidence

Decision fatigue is real and makes evening choices like dinner harder than the whole workday.

Research Quality: The underlying 'depletion' idea failed its preregistered 23-lab replication (d=0.04, CI includes zero) [3]. The best-known field evidence, judges' parole decisions [4], is disputed on case-ordering grounds [5]. No ADHD-specific evidence was found. Rated Unsupported as stated; evening tiredness is real, but 'decision fatigue' as a mechanism is not established.
Point 3 Partially supported Moderate confidence

Hyperfocus isn't a superpower; it is trouble switching attention ('the brakes don't work').

Research Quality: Adults with ADHD show a medium set-shifting deficit on lab tasks [6] and report hyperfocus more [7], but no study shows hyperfocus is itself a switching deficit, and the construct is poorly defined [8]. Studies linking measured hyperfocus episodes to switch costs would strengthen it.
Point 4 Insufficient evidence Moderate confidence

Saying 'What?' and then answering before the other person repeats it is the ADHD brain processing words a beat late.

Research Quality: A common anecdote. No study of this specific pattern in ADHD was found; it more plausibly reflects a brief attention lapse than slow word processing. Lab studies of delayed comprehension after inattention would test it. The 'spotless house or relaxed nervous system' and planner lines are opinion or jokes, not checked.

Claim #F8·Partially supported·Moderate confidence
Point 1 Partially supported Moderate confidence

Exercise is good for the brain in ADHD.

Research Quality: An umbrella review of 45 meta-analyses found structured exercise improved core symptoms (SMD 0.50) and executive function (SMD 0.77) in children and adolescents with ADHD, but rated the certainty low and called it an adjunct, not a replacement [1]. Adult trials are fewer. Large preregistered adult RCTs would strengthen it.
Point 2 Unsupported Moderate confidence

Exercise helps Concerta (methylphenidate) reach the brain effectively (the poster's doctor, as quoted).

Research Quality: No pharmacokinetic study showing exercise increases methylphenidate delivery to the brain was found in PubMed. The closest work measured blood dopamine and growth-hormone responses to exercise with and without methylphenidate in 20 children and did not test drug delivery [2]. Possibly a misremembered paraphrase; worth asking the doctor what they meant.

About this report

9 cards (10 posts, 31 points: 19 new, 12 pointing back to earlier verdicts) checked with the Scientific Evidence Evaluator framework. The best-sourced post was the genetics one: the 2025 Nature rare-variant study (MAP1A, ANO8, ANK2; odds ratios up to 15) and the 74% heritability figure are both accurate (Supported, GRADE moderate). Exercise helping ADHD symptoms is Partially supported (low certainty), but the quoted doctor's line that exercise helps Concerta reach the brain has no evidence. Unsupported: 'decision fatigue is real' (the depletion effect failed a 23-lab replication), 'researchers confirm' lateness is neurological time blindness with no internal time sense, and 'just break it down doesn't work for ADHD', which CBT trials contradict. Many other points repeat earlier verdicts and point back to them.

How claims were evaluated. Evidence hierarchy: Systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Level 1), RCTs (2), cohort (3), case-control (4), cross-sectional (5), case reports and expert opinion (6). Guidelines and clinical reviews are named as such. GRADE certainty: Our rating of the body of evidence for this specific claim: randomised evidence starts High and observational starts Low. Ratings go down for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness (e.g. non-ADHD or lab-only samples), imprecision and publication bias, and up for large effects. Verdicts: Supported: strong, consistent, high-quality evidence. Partially supported: some solid evidence, but the claim overgeneralises or has important caveats. Insufficient evidence: too little good-quality research to judge. Unsupported: evidence is absent for the claim as stated, or contradicts it (flagged as contradicted). Confidence is our confidence in the verdict, which can be high even when GRADE is low (e.g. a well-hedged claim).

Verdict changes: No earlier thread was extended (no new self-replies to older chains; body-doubling root 2109718323529183712 is still 1/2 only), so no earlier verdict changed. Repeat points link to their earlier verdicts.

Method. Posts were pulled with the X API. Self-reply parts were reconstructed via conversation_id and reply links and quoted verbatim, numbered in order. Reference metadata, corrections and retractions were checked against Crossref and PubMed, and open-access full texts were read. GRADE ratings are our own per-claim judgement unless stated.
