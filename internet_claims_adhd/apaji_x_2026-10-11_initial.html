1/4 – Brain scans proved why emotions hit at 100% for people with ADHD, and you can't stop them. You are not too sensitive or dramatic: 1/4

4/4 – Brain scans show that ADHD RSD lights up the same pain centers as getting physically hurt. Your feelings aren't over the top. They are neurologically amplified. 1/6 – Stop calling yourself "too sensitive." That unanswered text didn't hurt because you're dramatic. Your ADHD brain feels rejection at a different volume. They replied "ok." You were described with the wrong map. 4/4

Claim #5·Partially supported·Moderate confidence
Research Quality: Claim: shame floods the prefrontal cortex with cortisol and impairs executive function, so self-compassion restores it. Level 1 meta-analyses in the general population (GRADE low, emerging consensus) show social-evaluative stress raises cortisol [1] and slightly impairs working memory (g=−0.20) [2], and animal work links stress to prefrontal impairment [3]. However, cortisol alone does not reproduce these effects, and the shame → self-compassion chain has never been tested in ADHD. RCTs of self-compassion in ADHD, measuring cortisol and executive function, would strengthen it.

7/7 – So "if you really loved it, you'd do it" was never the test. You loved it the whole time. Starting was the broken part. The guitar still won't play itself, and the people around you get to wonder. But the answer was never more love. It was fewer steps.

ACTUALLY, YOU ARE ALLOWED TO ASK FOR ADHD WORKPLACE ACCOMMODATIONS...

1/7 – ACTUALLY, YOU ARE ALLOWED TO ASK FOR ADHD WORKPLACE ACCOMMODATIONS... WITHOUT EVER SAYING THE WORD ADHD. Most people with ADHD never ask. They just burn out quietly instead. 1/7

2/7 – 1. Most of these don't need a diagnosis on the table. "I do my best work with written follow-ups." "I focus better with headphones on." Framed as working preferences, a lot of these get a yes and nobody asks why. Formal accommodations through HR can ask for paperwork. The everyday ones usually don't. You do not owe anyone your full neurological history to get the conditions that let you do your job well.

3/7 – 2. Written instructions are a normal thing to ask for. Your manager rattles off six steps in the hallway. By step four, step one is gone. ADHD working memory can't reliably hold a multi-step verbal briefing. Asking for it in writing is not laziness. It's removing a barrier a neurotypical colleague never has to deal with. One line does it: "Can you drop that in a message so I get every step right?"

4/7 – 3. So are headphones, or a quieter desk. Open-plan noise is a documented ADHD barrier. Every conversation two desks over pulls attention off the work. This is not a preference. It's a reasonable adjustment, and it can be the difference between functional and non-functional.

5/7 – 4. Someone will call it asking for special favors. But an accommodation is not an advantage. It is the removal of a disadvantage. A ramp isn't a special favor for a wheelchair user. It's equal access. ADHD accommodations work exactly the same way.

6/7 – 5. Without them, the job costs double. The harder the work, the more these matter. Without them, half your energy goes into fighting the environment instead of doing the work. That gap is where burnout comes from.

7/7 – So the headphones aren't a favor, and the written recap isn't a weakness. They're what lets the work you're actually good at show up. Some managers will still roll their eyes. And asking can still feel like admitting you can't keep up. It isn't. It's the reason you can.

Claim #13·Partially supported·Moderate confidence
Research Quality: Claim: open-plan noise is a documented ADHD barrier, and headphones or a quiet desk help. A pre-registered Level 1 meta-analysis found a small benefit of white or pink noise in ADHD (g=0.25, no publication bias) [1], but results vary by symptom profile [2], and the evidence is lab-based and mostly in children (GRADE low, emerging consensus). Red flags: indirectness to adult offices, and the noise-benefit hypothesis comes from Söderlund's own group. Workplace studies of adults with ADHD would strengthen it.

About this report

18 claims re-evaluated. The attention-grabbing headlines (“BREAKING”, “brain scans prove”, “researchers confirm”) were the least reliable part: none held up. Two verdicts changed (claims 11 and 12), both because the practical tip itself has never been tested. The second run (8:06 AM) is appended to the Markdown report and has its own page.

How claims were evaluated. Evidence hierarchy: Systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Level 1), RCTs (2), cohort (3), case-control (4), cross-sectional (5), case reports and expert opinion (6). Guidelines and clinical reviews are named as such. GRADE certainty: Our rating of the body of evidence for this specific claim: randomised evidence starts High and observational starts Low. Ratings go down for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness (e.g. non-ADHD or lab-only samples), imprecision and publication bias, and up for large effects. Verdicts: Supported: strong, consistent, high-quality evidence. Partially supported: some solid evidence, but the claim overgeneralises or has important caveats. Insufficient evidence: too little good-quality research to judge. Unsupported: evidence is absent for the claim as stated, or contradicts it (flagged as contradicted). Confidence is our confidence in the verdict, which can be high even when GRADE is low (e.g. a well-hedged claim).

Verdict changes: 2 changed in substance; 11 relabelled onto the four-category scale with no change in substance (Not supported or Contradicted → Unsupported; Partly → Partially supported).
